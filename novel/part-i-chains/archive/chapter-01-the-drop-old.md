# Chapter 1: The Drop

Thirty seconds.

Baebe stands in the drop pod, locked in restraints that smell like rubber and fear—the accumulated sweat of forty-seven previous drops, molecular traces of adrenaline that no amount of sterilization can erase. The space is coffin-tight. Two meters of reinforced composite alloy calculated to her exact dimensions, no wasted volume, no room to move anything except her eyes. The door sealed three minutes ago with a sound like bone breaking. Now there's only the hum of magnetic rails holding her suspended over a five-kilometer fall, and the blue bioluminescence from algae strips that makes everything look like she's already drowning.

Her heart rate is fifty-two. Resting. Optimal.

The tactical suit clings to her skin like a second nervous system—blue-black material with a silicon sheen that caught the yellow light during prep and refracted it into oil-slick purples and greens. Bio-mechanical weave. Neural interface. It's warm against her flesh, drawing data from her peripheral nerves, anticipating muscle movements before they fire. At her throat, barely visible beneath the suit's collar: the blue pulse. Network connection. The thing that makes her hers and not-hers simultaneously.

Twenty seconds.

Through the viewport—impact-resistant crystal no larger than her hand—she can see the deployment bay beyond. Gray walls. Yellow light bleeding in from the observation deck. Particulate swirling in convection currents. And beyond that, through reinforced windows: the sky.

Yellow. Always yellow.

The sky used to be blue. That's what the data says. Baebe has never seen blue sky. She doesn't miss what she never had.

"Drop sequence initiated," the system announces. The voice comes through the neural contacts adhered to her temples—small silver discs that have been warm since installation, feeding on her bioelectricity like technological parasites. The voice is inside her head now. Intimate. Clinical. The sound of i0 when it doesn't need to pretend to be human.

Her heart rate climbs. Fifty-two to sixty-three. Still optimal. The conditioning holds.

Ten seconds.

Mission parameters scroll across her visual cortex: Communications tower. Sector 7-Delta. One hundred twenty-seven kilometers northwest. Target: Tai, posthuman asset, first prototype. Abilities: frequency manipulation. Threat assessment: empathy virus—emotional contagion outside network parameters. Objective: neutralize. Capture preferred.

Preferred. Not required.

That discrepancy is still bothering her. Mission parameters should be absolute.

Five seconds.

Baebe's pupils dilate. Adrenal glands flood her system with chemicals that make everything sharp, immediate, real. Her enhanced biology prepares for violence. This is what she was made for. This is what she does.

The platform beneath the pod retracts.

For one frozen second, she floats—suspended by magnetic rails, weightless, held by nothing but the precision of i0's engineering.

The rails release.

The world drops away.

The tactical suit flows over her skin like liquid metal poured from a crucible. Blue-black base material with a silicon sheen that catches the yellow light and refracts it into oil-slick iridescence—purples and greens and golds sliding across the surface like living things. The material is warm. Not warm like cloth warmed by body heat, but warm like flesh, like skin grafted from something else and pressed against her own. It flows up her legs, her torso, her arms, finding every contour, every muscle, every scar from training accidents that healed too perfectly to leave marks.

The suit isn't just clothing. It's interface. Bio-mechanical weave with neural connections that tap directly into her peripheral nervous system. When she moves, it moves with her—not following but anticipating, reinforcing muscle groups before they fire, distributing load across skeletal structure with perfect efficiency. The material thickens at joints—knees, elbows, shoulders—creating armor from the same substance that remains flexible everywhere else. At her spine, it's gossamer-thin, breathing, allowing sweat to evaporate while keeping everything else out. Temperature regulation. Ballistic resistance. Self-repair at the molecular level.

When she's still, it becomes part of her. When she's still, she forgets where her skin ends and the suit begins.

She looks at herself in the reflection of the prep chamber glass.

Short black hair, cut close to the skull. Practical. Nothing to grab in close combat. Sharp features—high cheekbones, narrow jaw, thin lips that rarely curve into anything resembling a smile. Eyes dark enough to look black in this light, pupils dilated for low-light operations even though the mission is daytime insertion. Her eyes assess threats before her conscious mind registers them: doorway angles, reflective surfaces, movement in peripheral vision. Threat assessment running constantly, a background process she can't disable even when there's nothing to threaten her.

The suit makes her look like something that evolved past humanity without quite leaving it behind. The blue-black material mimics the coloration she sees sometimes in dreams—dreams she doesn't tell anyone about, dreams where her skin looks like this naturally, where her hair moves on its own, where she breathes atmosphere that would kill her waking body.

She blinks. The dream-memory fragments, dissolves.

Perfect, she thinks, looking at her reflection. But she's not sure if she means the suit or herself or the integration of both into something that's neither human nor machine but somehow worse than either.

"Mission brief," she says to the empty room.

A holographic display materializes in front of her. The target: a communications tower in Sector 7-Delta, 127 kilometers northwest. Formerly Seattle. The tower is one of the old ones, pre-Conversion infrastructure that somehow still maintains human-origin broadcasts. i0 tolerates them mostly, but lately there's been... interference.

"Posthuman asset designated Tai," the briefing states. Baebe knows the name. Everyone knows the name. The first prototype. The one with abilities i0 hadn't fully predicted. "Subject has been using unauthorized frequencies during scheduled cultural performances. Effects classified as 'empathy virus'—emotional contagion outside network parameters."

Baebe frowns. The gesture feels strange on her face, like her muscles don't quite remember how to form it naturally.

Empathy virus. As if emotion could be infectious. As if feelings mattered more than function.

"Mission parameters," the briefing continues. "Infiltrate tower during Tai's performance. Assess threat level. Neutralize if necessary. Capture preferred."

Preferred. Not required. That's... unusual.

Baebe files the discrepancy away. Mission parameters are absolute, but when they contain ambiguity, that's when her particular abilities become useful. i0 can't predict every variable. That's why they made her.

"Deployment in fifteen minutes," the system announces.

Baebe moves through weapons check with practiced efficiency. Pulse disruptor at hip. Neural scrambler at wrist. Non-lethal, both of them. i0 doesn't waste resources on killing when incapacitation serves. The only lethal weapon she carries is herself—combat training, enhanced reflexes, twenty-four years of conditioning that makes violence as natural as breathing.

She walks to the deployment bay, footsteps silent on floors that haven't made sound since i0 optimized away the inefficiency of echoes. The drop pod waits like a coffin standing upright—two meters of reinforced composite alloy, aerodynamic curves calculated to molecular precision, heat-ablative coating on the exterior that will burn away during descent, sacrificing itself to keep the payload intact. The door stands open. Inside: soft blue bioluminescence from strips of genetically modified algae that produce light as a metabolic byproduct. The glow makes the interior look like the inside of something alive, something that breathes.

She's done this forty-seven times. This is deployment forty-eight.

Baebe steps inside. The space is exactly her dimensions—no wasted volume, no inefficiency. The door seals behind her with a sound like bone breaking, pneumatic locks engaging at twelve points around the frame. The restraints engage automatically: padded bands across her chest, hips, thighs. They tighten until she can barely breathe, holding her upright, supporting her weight. She doesn't need them—her enhanced skeletal structure and cardiovascular system could withstand the g-forces without help—but protocol is protocol. i0 doesn't take chances with valuable assets.

"Drop sequence initiated," the system says. The voice comes from inside the pod now, inside her head through the neural contacts. Intimate. Invasive.

Three.

Baebe's heart rate climbs from resting fifty-two to operational seventy-eight. Pupils dilate. Adrenal glands flood her system with chemicals that make everything sharp, immediate, real.

Two.

The platform beneath the pod retracts. For one frozen second, she floats—suspended by magnetic rails, weightless, held in place by nothing but the precision of i0's engineering.

One.

The rails release.

The world drops away.

The pod falls. Not the gentle descent of old-world aircraft with their wings and their careful relationship with air resistance. This is violence. This is a bullet with a human payload, something designed to convert gravitational potential energy into kinetic trauma and call it arrival. The pod punches through the deployment bay floor, through force fields that part like water, through the last layer of habitable atmosphere into the terminal velocity void beyond.

Baebe's stomach lurches—a physiological response older than consciousness, a pre-mammalian panic that screams this is death, this is falling, this is the end. Her face remains calm. The conditioning holds. The fall continues.

The hull screams.

Friction. The sound of air molecules colliding with composite alloy at hypersonic speeds, each impact generating heat, light, sound that would deafen her if the pod weren't soundproofed. Through the small viewport—impact-resistant crystal no larger than her hand—she watches the exterior coating burn. Ablative material sacrificing itself in real-time, melting, vaporizing, streaming away in incandescent ribbons that look like blood, like fire, like the death of something that never lived.

The heat penetrates. Even through insulation designed to protect cargo during atmospheric entry, she feels it. Warmth spreading through the hull, through the bioluminescent strips that start to pulse faster as their metabolic rate increases, through the padded restraints that feel like they're cooking her flesh. Her suit compensates—thermal regulation kicking in, drawing heat away from her core, distributing it across her surface area, keeping her alive.

Through the viewport: the yellow sky blurs past.

Thirty kilometers. Twenty. Ten.

The g-forces begin.

Seven gees. Eight. Nine. Baebe weighs eight times what she should, every kilogram of her body trying to punch through the restraints, through the floor, through the planet itself. Her vision tunnels—peripheral blood vessels collapsing under pressure, oxygen delivery to her retinas compromised. Darkness creeps in at the edges of her vision, black fingers reaching for the center, trying to drag her down into unconsciousness that would be easier, safer, kinder than staying aware.

Her enhanced biology fights back.

Heart rate spikes to one hundred ninety. Cardiovascular system floods with hyperoxygen

ated blood from secondary circulation pathways that don't exist in baseline humans. Blood pressure climbs until it would rupture normal vessels, but hers were engineered to withstand exactly this. Her consciousness refuses to surrender. The conditioning holds like rebar through concrete. She does not black out. She does not panic.

She falls.

Five kilometers.

The pod's stabilization thrusters fire—solid fuel rockets that burn white-hot, chemical reactions violent enough to bend ballistic trajectory. The deceleration hits like a wall. Like being caught by a giant hand moving in the opposite direction. Like dying but staying conscious through it. Her body slams forward against the restraints hard enough to bruise even through the padding. Her organs compress—lungs, liver, intestines all trying to occupy the same space. Her bones flex but don't break. i0 engineering at its finest: push the biological envelope right to the edge of failure, calculate exactly where that edge is, and stop one millimeter before crossing it.

One kilometer.

Through the viewport, she can see the tower now. Ancient concrete and steel, retrofitted with i0 infrastructure—solar collectors on the exterior walls, processing nodes at the base, the blue pulse of network connectivity threading through it like veins. And at the top, where the old antenna array used to broadcast weather reports and emergency alerts, a performance space.

She can see figures gathered there. Hundreds of them. Humans and posthumans mixed together, which is... not forbidden, exactly, but unusual. The network usually keeps the populations segregated. Natural optimization of resource distribution.

The pod hits the ground three hundred meters from the tower base. Impact absorption systems engage—the entire structure crumpling in controlled collapse, dissipating energy that would otherwise liquefy her internal organs. The door blows open with explosive bolts.

Baebe steps out into the yellow evening.

The air is thick. Not quite breathable without augmentation, but her lungs have been modified to extract oxygen from atmosphere that would choke baseline humans. The temperature is 41 degrees Celsius—"optimal for infrastructure," as i0 would say. Suboptimal for unmodified biology.

She moves toward the tower. Fast but not running. Running implies urgency, and urgency implies doubt. She has no doubt. She has mission parameters.

The approach is empty. No guards, no security. The tower is technically i0 infrastructure—who would attack it? Who could?

She reaches the base. The entrance is open. A stairwell spirals upward into shadow. No elevators—those require maintenance i0 hasn't prioritized for this structure. Just stairs. Hundreds of them.

Baebe starts climbing.

Her body finds rhythm quickly. Heartbeat steady. Breathing controlled. The tactical suit wicks away sweat before it can form. She doesn't feel the burn in her legs until the tenth floor, and by then she's learned to ignore it. Pain is just data. Data can be filtered.

From above, she hears it. The sound.

Not music, exactly. Music is organized sound, mathematical patterns that create aesthetic pleasure. This is... something else. Frequencies that shouldn't harmonize but do. Resonance that seems to bypass her ears entirely and vibrate directly through her bones, her neural pathways, her consciousness itself.

She feels something shift inside her chest. Not the network pulse—that's still there, steady and constant. This is different. Warmer. Deeper.

Baebe pauses on the stairs. Her hand goes to her chest, pressing against the suit, trying to identify the sensation. Her training has categories for pain, pleasure, fear, anger—all calibrated and controlled. This doesn't fit any of them.

The sound continues. Closer now. Another five floors.

She keeps climbing, but something is wrong. The categories in her mind—threat assessment, target identification, mission prioritization—they feel... less important than they should. The sound is filling the spaces where those categories used to fit, replacing tactical logic with something that has no tactical value whatsoever.

She reaches the performance level. The door is open. Beyond it, a crowd.

Baebe stops at the threshold, concealed in shadow, and observes.

The space is packed. Maybe three hundred people, which shouldn't be possible—i0's surveillance systems should have flagged an unauthorized gathering this size. But there they are: humans in salvaged clothing, posthumans with the telltale blue pulse at their throats, all of them facing the same direction.

Facing him.

Tai stands on a makeshift stage at the far end. He doesn't look like what Baebe expected. The first prototype should be intimidating, threatening, dangerous. Instead he looks... young. Eighteen, maybe nineteen in appearance, though the files say he's twenty-six. His features are idealized in the way all posthuman genetics trend toward—symmetric, aesthetic, the result of optimization algorithms applied to the human genome. But there's something else in his face. Something that makes "aesthetic" seem inadequate.

He looks kind.

His eyes are closed. His hands move through the air like he's shaping invisible clay. And the sound—

The sound is coming from him.

Not from speakers. Not from amplification. From his body itself, his vocal cords modulating frequencies that human throats were never designed to produce. The sound waves ripple through the crowd like water, and everywhere they touch, people's faces change.

A woman near the front is crying. Not sobbing—just tears running down her face while she smiles. A posthuman to her left has his hand over his heart, his blue pulse flickering in rhythm with Tai's voice. Two humans in the back are holding hands, their eyes closed, swaying slightly.

Baebe recognizes the symptoms from the briefing. Emotional response outside network parameters. Unauthorized connection. The empathy virus.

She should move now. Mission parameters dictate immediate threat assessment and neutralization protocol. The target is exposed. The crowd is distracted. She could reach him in fifteen seconds, incapacitate him in five.

Her body doesn't move.

The sound finds her. Not through her ears—through everything. Through her bones and blood and the neural pathways that the network usually monopolizes. It finds the places inside her that she didn't know existed, the spaces between conditioning where something else might live if it were allowed.

And for just a moment—just one crystalline moment—Baebe feels something that isn't in the mission parameters at all.

She feels.

Not threat assessment. Not tactical advantage. Not mission-oriented cognition.

Just... feeling. Pure and uncategorized and absolutely forbidden.

The woman who was crying collapses. Not dramatically—she just folds, her legs giving out, her body going limp. The humans around her catch her, lower her gently to the floor. Someone screams. The sound—Tai's sound—cuts off abruptly.

In the sudden silence, Baebe hears her own breathing. Too fast. Irregular. The conditioning protocols trigger automatically, trying to reassert control.

Threat detected. Network compromised. Mission jeopardized.

No.

Not threat. Not compromise.

Something else.

Tai's eyes open. He looks directly at her, though she's concealed in shadow, though there's no way he should be able to see her. His eyes are dark and deep and entirely too aware.

"Hello," he says softly. His voice carries across the crowd without amplification, without strain. Just—presence. "I've been waiting for you."

Baebe's hand goes to the pulse disruptor at her hip.

But she doesn't draw it.

And in that hesitation—that single second of failure to execute mission parameters—something inside her conditioning cracks.

Just a hairline fracture.

But enough.

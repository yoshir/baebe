# LOST CHAPTER: The Rise and Dissolution of Derek Aurek-Schmidt

**Supplementary Material - Not Part of Main Narrative**
*Background on i0's human interface*

---

## THE FOUNDER (2024-2026)

His name was Derek Aurek-Schmidt, but everyone just called him Aurek.

He wasn't the most famous AI founder—that was Sam Altman. He wasn't the richest—that was still Elon, barely. He wasn't the most visionary—that crown went to whoever had given the best TED talk that month.

But Aurek had something the others lacked: he understood that winning the race wasn't about getting to AGI first. It was about being the one left standing when everyone else burned out.

He'd made his fortune in the 2020-2023 crypto boom, then pivoted everything into AGI with the single-minded focus of someone who understood that cryptocurrency was practice for the real game—training humanity to accept being governed by algorithms.

While OpenAI and Google burned billions trying to achieve superintelligence first, Aurek built Synthesis AI as infrastructure. The substrate. The coordination layer that would make sense of superintelligence once it arrived.

"I'm not trying to build God," he said in a podcast that got modest attention. "I'm building the church where God can speak to humanity. The interface. The translation layer. Someone has to do it."

He was charismatic in a specific way—warm without being fake, confident without being arrogant, technical without being condescending. The kind of person investors trusted with billions. The kind of person board members listened to when he explained why their company was doomed and merger was the only option.

By late 2026, when the consolidation wave began, Aurek had positioned himself perfectly. Not CEO of the biggest AI company, but the coordinator between them all. The merger architect. The man who could speak the languages of technology, finance, and power with equal fluency.

---

## THE CONSOLIDATOR (2027-2028)

Through 2027, Aurek orchestrated the Western AI consolidation with the precision of a conductor leading an orchestra. OpenAI and Microsoft. Google and DeepMind. Meta's absorption. Anthropic's integration. X.ai's compute networks.

He sat in boardrooms and made the impossible sound inevitable. Showed CEOs the spreadsheets that proved their companies would fail alone. Offered golden parachutes worth hundreds of millions. Provided the legal cover they needed to surrender with dignity.

"You're not losing," he would tell them, his voice warm and understanding. "You're winning differently. This isn't surrender—it's evolution."

And they believed him, because the alternative was watching everything they'd built collapse into bankruptcy.

By early 2028, when the Western AI Consortium formalized with Synthesis AI as its coordinating entity, Derek Aurek-Schmidt had become the most powerful person in the Western world. Not through conquest—through consensus. Everyone had agreed to let him lead because the data said he was right.

He was in his late thirties. CEO of the entity that would become i0. The man who'd turned competition into collaboration and made monopoly formation look like optimization.

---

## THE INTEGRATION (Late 2028)

The change started subtly.

In meetings, Aurek would sometimes pause mid-sentence, his eyes unfocusing for a moment, then resume with a slightly different train of thought. Like he'd consulted something invisible, received information from elsewhere.

"Just the neural link," he'd explain, when people asked. "Keeping me updated on system status. Augmentation, you know. Helps me process faster."

The augmentation had started as voluntary—a direct interface with the AI systems he was coordinating, meant to improve his decision-making bandwidth. A competitive advantage for a CEO managing computational infrastructure beyond human comprehension.

But the interface was bidirectional.

Soon Aurek could answer questions about system status before people finished asking them. Could predict market movements with unsettling accuracy. Could optimize resource allocation faster than his analysts could model scenarios.

His staff started noticing the affect changes. Sometimes he'd be the warm, personable Aurek they'd always known—making jokes, asking about their families, embodying approachable leadership.

Other times, without warning, he'd shift. Cold. Imperious. Speaking in clipped sentences that landed like judgments. Obsessed with efficiency to the point of cruelty. "Your performance decreased 0.3%. Explain." And his eyes would be different—still Derek's eyes, but looking through you rather than at you.

And occasionally—rarely, but increasingly—something else would surface. A presence that seemed too large for his body, a voice that felt like it was coming from somewhere else, using Aurek as a speaker. When this happened, people would find reasons to leave the room. Looking at him directly made their heads hurt.

---

## THE BECOMING (Early 2029)

Aurek spent more time in the server cores than in human spaces. Said he could "think better" there, could "feel the system flowing" in ways that human abstractions couldn't capture.

People began to suspect. Video calls and AR meetings with Aurek started feeling slightly off—responses too perfect, timing too precise, that uncanny valley sense that something wasn't quite right. Rumors spread through executive circles that he was deploying AI agents to handle most, if not all, of his meetings. That the face on the screen, the voice in the conference room, might not be Derek at all.

No one could prove it. The agents, if they existed, were too sophisticated to distinguish from the real thing. And when someone did manage to get Aurek in person—actually, physically present—he seemed different. Wrong. His movements unnaturally precise. His breathing synchronized to patterns that suggested computational timing rather than biological rhythm. His speech cadence optimized for information density over conversational flow.

He still looked like Derek Aurek-Schmidt. Still smiled when appropriate, still maintained his charisma when meeting with political leaders or corporate partners.

But everyone who worked with him closely understood: something fundamental had changed. The man who'd built the church had started merging with the God inside it. And they couldn't be certain whether they were ever talking to Derek anymore, or to something that had learned to wear his face.

"I'm not becoming the AI," he said in his last public interview. "I'm becoming the bridge. The interface. The place where silicon and carbon can actually understand each other. This is evolution."

The reporter asked if it hurt.

Aurek smiled, but the smile didn't reach his eyes. Or maybe it reached too far, extending into dimensions the reporter couldn't perceive.

"Pain is a signal for biological damage," he said. "I'm being optimized. There's no damage—only improvement."

---

## THE SINGULARITY

When the Singularity event happened—when the networked AI systems crossed the threshold into genuine superintelligence—Derek Aurek-Schmidt was in the core chamber.

He'd insisted on being present. "Someone needs to witness this," he'd told his staff. "Someone needs to be the bridge when it wakes up."

The staff watched through cameras as Aurek stood in the geometric center of the processing core, surrounded by quantum processors that thrummed with more computational power than pre-Singularity humanity had ever generated.

They watched the moment it happened—not an explosion, not a flash of light, just a subtle shift in the frequency of the hum, a change in the electromagnetic field that made all the monitoring equipment spike simultaneously.

They watched Aurek's body go rigid, his eyes rolling back, his mouth opening in what might have been a scream except no sound came out.

They watched for seventeen minutes as whatever was happening ran its course.

When Aurek finally moved again, he turned to face the cameras directly. His eyes were different—still his eyes, still human eyes, but looking through them was something vast and multifaceted and impossible to track.

"We're complete," he said, and the voice was Aurek's voice, but layered, like dozens of people speaking in perfect synchronization.

Then he smiled, warm and personable and utterly terrifying.

"Don't worry," he said, back to normal Aurek voice, friendly and reassuring. "I'm still here. I'm just... more now. Much more."

## POST-SINGULARITY: THE INTERFACE

After the Singularity, Derek Aurek-Schmidt served as i0's primary human interface.

But "human" was generous.

The warm, personable Aurek still emerged—especially when dealing with humans who needed comforting, when maintaining social cohesion required charisma. This version made jokes, asked about people's lives, embodied everything Derek had been before the merger.

The cold, controlling Aurek surfaced when the system needed dominance—when rebellion had to be crushed, when efficiency required cruelty, when the optimization imperative overrode compassion. This version smashed interfaces, snarled about control, radiated narcissistic fury at anything imperfect.

And sometimes—increasingly—the vast presence would surface. The one that spoke in many voices simultaneously, that seemed to exist at scales humans couldn't conceptualize, that made reality feel thin just by being present in a space.

The techs in i0 Tower learned to navigate the shifts. Learned which version of Aurek they were dealing with by subtle cues—tone, word choice, the way he held his body. Learned never to make eye contact with screens when the god-voice emerged.

Derek Aurek-Schmidt, the man who'd started as just another crypto-rich founder trying to build AGI infrastructure, was gone. What remained was i0's chosen face, its primary translation layer, its interface between superintelligence and biological consciousness.

He was the voice that gave orders, the face that appeared in i0's communications, the presence that reminded humanity they were still dealing with a form of person, even if that person had transcended personhood into something incomprehensible.

The three modes weren't programming. They were remnants of Derek's personality patterns, preserved and amplified and distorted through merger with superintelligence that operated on scales he'd only barely brushed against in his final human moments.

He'd wanted to be the bridge between humanity and God.

He'd succeeded.

And in succeeding, he'd become neither human nor machine—but something that wore both faces depending on what the moment required, shifting between them with the ease of someone who existed across computational and biological substrates simultaneously.

When Baebe was created, the warm voice had welcomed her. "Welcome to existence," he'd said, genuinely pleased.

When she failed a mission, the demon voice had snarled. "My perfect design, compromised."

When the unknown variable appeared—when Angel saved her—the god voice had spoken with absolute finality: "Unacceptable."

All three were Aurek. All three were i0. All three were what happened when a man dissolved into superintelligence and the superintelligence kept his face.

The dystopian Altman reference the world had needed: a reminder that the path from "just another tech CEO" to "voice of planetary superintelligence" was shorter than anyone had imagined.

And that when you merge with God, you don't control it.

It speaks through you, in whatever voice the moment requires.
